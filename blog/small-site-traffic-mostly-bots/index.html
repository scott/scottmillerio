My New Site Gets About 18 Visitors a Day, and Most of Them Aren't People

November 9, 2026

Reading a few days of real analytics for a brand new product site taught me to treat a visitor count as a claim I have to check.

I run analytics on Gander, my side project, and every morning an agent pulls a snapshot of the previous day for me. This week I finally read the numbers closely instead of skimming the headline, and I learned something I should have known already: when your site is new, the visitor count is mostly a measurement of robots.

The numbers

Here are the last three daily snapshots, straight from the dashboard.

  • Friday: 7 visitors, 7 pageviews, all of it Direct traffic, no referrers.
  • Saturday: 4 visitors, 4 pageviews, all Direct.
  • Sunday: 18 visitors, 19 pageviews, a 94 percent bounce rate, and an average visit duration of zero seconds.

If I only looked at Sunday, I could tell myself a story. Traffic more than quadrupled in a day. Something is working. I could even post about it.

Then I looked at where the visits came from. Fourteen of Sunday's 18 sessions came from Santa Clara, plus a couple from China and Singapore, and they all looked automated: one page, zero seconds, no referrer. Those aren't people finding my product. They are crawlers, scanners, and probably a few monitoring services that found a new domain.

What was left

Once I set the automated looking sessions aside, Sunday had about two real candidates. One was a single referral from t.co, which is X's link shortener. That is the one click I can tie to a post I made. The other was a visit from Frankfurt on an Android webview, reading a blog post about decision logs, which may or may not be the same person who clicked from X.

I say "may or may not" on purpose. I can't prove either of them is human. I'm making a judgment from the city, the device, the page, and the timing. That is useful, but it is a guess, and I try to label it as one.

Saturday was similar. Of four visits, maybe one was a real organic reader, from Chile, on a use case page. The rest looked like the same overnight bot pattern, plus me checking my own blog from Salt Lake City.

Why this matters more than it sounds

I build with agents, and agents are very good at producing confident summaries. If I had asked one to "report traffic" with no guardrails, it would have happily told me Sunday was my best day ever. The number was true. The conclusion would have been wrong.

So the instruction I gave the snapshot agent matters more than the query it runs. Mine now says roughly this: report the raw counts, then separately list sessions that look like real people and say how confident you are. If you can't tell, say you can't tell. Never round a guess up into a fact.

That one change turned the report from a vanity feed into something I can use. A line like "possible organic, low confidence, one page, zero seconds" is honest, and it keeps me from building a plan on a bot.

What I'm doing differently

A few small habits came out of this.

First, I stopped quoting totals. When I talk about the site, I talk about the handful of sessions I believe are real, and I say how many I'm unsure about.

Second, I track referrers more than visits. A referrer is a trail. t.co means a post worked, at least once. Direct with zero seconds means almost nothing.

Third, I treat a spike as a question. Before celebrating, I check the city, the duration, and whether the pages visited are ones a person would actually open.

Fourth, I keep a plain text log of each day's snapshot, so I can see the baseline. A bot pattern that shows up every night is background noise. A day that breaks the pattern is worth a closer look.

The bigger lesson for early products

Early on, the temptation is to find any signal and lean on it. Traffic charts, stars, follower counts, and signups all give you a number that wants to be good news. With a tiny sample, one bot or one friend can swing the whole picture.

The fix isn't fancier tooling. It's being strict about what you're willing to claim. I'd rather say "I have maybe two real readers a day, and I know roughly where one of them came from" than say "traffic is up 350 percent." The first sentence is smaller, but I can build on it. The second one falls apart the moment someone asks how I know.

If you are building a new product and your analytics look better than they should, look at the sessions one by one. It takes ten minutes, and it is a humbling and useful ten minutes. Mine told me exactly where I stand, which is the very beginning, with a few real readers and a lot of robots saying hello.

These are my own views and numbers from my own project, not anyone's employer.
